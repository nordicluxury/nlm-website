The Vantage · Infrastructure · Finance · October 2026

Europe's Biggest
Cost Overruns

Five megaprojects. Approximately €80 billion beyond their original budgets. Ranked by cost multiplier — how many times over budget each one ran — not by total spend.

The Vantage · Nordic Luxury Media October 2026 10 min read

Major infrastructure projects across Europe have repeatedly exceeded their original budgets by multiples. Photo: Unsplash

Getting a major infrastructure project built in Europe at or near its original budget is the exception. Getting one built on schedule is rarer still. The five projects in this ranking represent the most extreme examples on the continent in recent decades: collectively, approximately €80 billion spent beyond what was promised at the point of approval.

Each project failed differently. Understanding why is worth more than the numbers alone.

Methodology

This ranking is ordered by cost multiplier: final or current cost divided by the original approved budget. A project that ran to 7x its estimate ranks above one that ran to 4x, regardless of which spent more in absolute terms.

The qualifying threshold is an original approved budget of €2 billion or more. Projects below that level are excluded. Only projects with a clearly defined original approved budget are included.

For ongoing projects (Stuttgart 21, Rail Baltica), the current cost figure is the most recent official estimate and may still rise. For Flamanville 3, the ranking uses the €23.7 billion figure calculated by France's Cour des Comptes in January 2025, in 2023 euros, including financing costs. EDF's own construction-only estimate of €13.2 billion is referenced in the text.

#5
Stuttgart 21 🇩🇪
Germany · Railway infrastructure
Original budget
€4.1bn
Current cost
€14.5bn
Multiplier
3.5x
Overrun
+€10.4bn

Stuttgart Hauptbahnhof — the station being replaced underground. The project has run since 2010 and will not fully open until 2033. Photo: Unsplash

Stuttgart 21 is a plan to replace Stuttgart's above-ground terminus station with a new underground through-station, dig approximately 56 kilometres of tunnels beneath the city, build a new station at Stuttgart Airport, and connect the whole thing to a high-speed rail line to Ulm. The project was formally approved in December 2009 at €4.09 billion. Construction began in 2010. The station has not opened.

In June 2026, Deutsche Bahn announced the latest revision: €14.5 billion total, up roughly €3 billion from the previous estimate. An internal audit had found serious failures in planning and project control. A technical building designed in 2013 no longer meets current operational requirements. Changes to power supply regulations required a complete system redesign that nobody had anticipated or budgeted for. DB CEO Evelyn Palla said publicly she was shocked by the findings.

The station will now open in stages between December 2027 and December 2033. The main underground Stuttgart Hauptbahnhof is planned for December 2031. Stuttgart city's courts confirmed in 2025 that the city carries none of the additional costs. Those fall entirely to Deutsche Bahn. The DB supervisory board has yet to approve the revised financing framework.

Long-duration projects always face a problem shorter ones do not. The regulations written in 2001 are not the regulations in force in 2026. The construction costs priced before two decades of inflation are not the costs of the current market. The technical specifications that passed review in 2013 do not necessarily meet what the regulator requires today. Stuttgart 21 was, in practical terms, a different project from the one approved at the start.

#4
Olkiluoto 3 🇫🇮
Finland · Nuclear power
Original budget
€3.2bn
Final cost
~€11bn
Multiplier
3.6x
Overrun
~+€8bn

Olkiluoto 3 is now Europe's largest operating nuclear reactor at 1,600 megawatts — fourteen years after it was supposed to be. Photo: Unsplash

In December 2003, Finnish nuclear utility TVO signed a fixed-price turnkey contract with the Areva-Siemens consortium to build a 1,600 megawatt EPR reactor at Olkiluoto on Finland's west coast. The price was €3.2 billion. The plant was scheduled for commercial operation by mid-2009. It reached commercial operation in April 2023, fourteen years late. Nuclear power experts have estimated the final cost at approximately €11 billion, about three times the original contract price.

Olkiluoto 3 was not primarily a planning failure. It was an industrial one. Nuclear construction in Western Europe had stopped for roughly two decades before work began on the reactor. Supply chains had contracted. The workforce capable of building to nuclear safety standards was smaller than the project required. When Areva arrived on site, quality control problems accumulated: welding deficiencies, documentation failures, components that did not meet regulatory specifications. Each required investigation, remediation and re-inspection. The delays compounded over years.

The legal proceedings between TVO and the Areva-Siemens consortium ran for nearly fifteen years. Areva claimed approximately €3.5 billion in additional costs from TVO. TVO countered with damages claims. The settlement in 2018 saw Areva pay €450 million to TVO.

Olkiluoto 3 is now Europe's largest operating nuclear reactor. It covers approximately 40% of Finland's electricity demand. The plant functions as designed. Getting there took fourteen extra years and roughly €8 billion beyond the contract price.

#3
Berlin Brandenburg Airport 🇩🇪
Germany · Aviation infrastructure
Original budget
~€2bn
Final cost
€7.3bn
Multiplier
3.7x
Overrun
+€5.3bn

Berlin Brandenburg Airport opened in October 2020, nine years behind schedule, at more than three times its original budget. Photo: Unsplash

Berlin Brandenburg Airport opened in October 2020. The original plan had it opening in October 2011. Nine years behind schedule and more than three times its original budget, BER spent the better part of a decade as the most scrutinised infrastructure failure in Germany.

The root cause was a decision made in 2003. The three shareholders in the airport company — the federal government, the state of Berlin and the state of Brandenburg — decided to have the airport company build the facility itself rather than hire a private developer. The airport company had competently operated Berlin's existing airports for years. Building a multibillion-euro project from scratch was categorically different work, and the organisation lacked the project management capability to do it.

The fire protection system became the most visible single failure. The system installed in the terminal did not meet safety certification requirements and had to be substantially redesigned and rebuilt. That required ripping out completed infrastructure and starting sections again. The resulting delay ran for years. Other problems compounded it: escalators built too short for their shafts, cable channels that intersected incorrectly, a control centre placed in the wrong location.

Three sovereign shareholders, each with partial oversight responsibility, and a supervisory board that changed composition repeatedly. Nobody was clearly in charge across all three simultaneously. Nobody could be clearly held accountable. In 2025, BER was named World's Most Improved Airport by the World Airport Awards. The airport now functions as designed.

#2
Rail Baltica 🇪🇪🇱🇻🇱🇹
Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania · Railway infrastructure
Original budget
€5.8bn
Current estimate
€23.8bn
Multiplier
4.1x
Overrun
+€18bn

Rail Baltica will connect Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius to the European standard-gauge rail network for the first time. No completion date exists for the full project. Photo: Unsplash

Rail Baltica is a planned European standard-gauge railway connecting Tallinn south through Riga and Vilnius to the Lithuanian-Polish border, integrating the three Baltic states into the wider European rail network for the first time. The project had a completion date of 2026. That became 2030. In January 2026, the European Court of Auditors confirmed that even the 2030 date for the first phase will not be met, and that no completion date exists for the full project.

€23.8 billion is more than twice the Estonian government's total annual expenditure, including pensions, healthcare, defence and infrastructure.

The total estimated cost rose from €5.8 billion at the 2020 audit to €23.8 billion in the 2026 report, based on 2023 price data. The auditors noted that the current figure is likely still conservative, given that detailed design studies cover only approximately one third of the total distance. Poland's Deputy Infrastructure Minister told the Financial Times in early 2026 that the project will not be completed before 2040.

The overrun reflects the difficulty of executing one project across three sovereign countries simultaneously. Design changes agreed in Latvia affect timelines in Estonia. Technical standard revisions between two governments require renegotiation with the third. A funding gap in one national stretch pauses construction elsewhere. The project was also split from one phase into two during implementation, and that change itself added significant cost.

Rail Baltica's strategic case has not weakened. The project was always partly about NATO logistics, specifically the ability to move troops and equipment rapidly to the alliance's eastern flank. That requirement became considerably more urgent from 2022 onwards. Whether the Baltic states and the EU can fund a project whose costs have risen 291% since initial estimates is a question that has no clear answer yet.

#1
Flamanville 3 🇫🇷
France · Nuclear power
Original budget
€3.3bn
Total cost (Cour des Comptes)
€23.7bn
Multiplier
7.2x
Overrun
+€20.4bn

Flamanville 3 connected to France's national electricity grid in December 2024, twelve years late. It is now France's most powerful reactor. Photo: Unsplash

Flamanville 3 is in a category apart. The Cour des Comptes, France's national auditing body, calculated the total cost of the project at €23.7 billion in its January 2025 report, expressed in 2023 euros. This figure includes construction costs and all financing costs accumulated over seventeen years of delays. EDF's own construction-only estimate is lower at €13.2 billion. The difference between the two is the cost of borrowing money to fund a project for nearly two decades longer than planned.

The original estimate in 2007 was €3.3 billion. The target completion date was 2012. The reactor was connected to France's national electricity grid on 21 December 2024, twelve years late. It reached 100% power output on 14 December 2025, generating 1,669 megawatts of gross electrical power. It is the most powerful reactor in France and the first new French reactor in 25 years.

The first EPR ran to 7.2x its original budget. Both Flamanville and Olkiluoto ran into the same reality: European nuclear construction capability had contracted severely through decades of inactivity after the 1980s.

The EPR is a Generation III pressurised water reactor designed by Framatome. Flamanville 3 was the first EPR built in France. The reactor at Olkiluoto 3 in Finland was built to the same design and encountered many of the same problems. The parallel is not a coincidence. The workforce, the supply chains, the quality management processes and the institutional knowledge needed to build a reactor to modern standards were all significantly smaller than required.

At Flamanville, the most serious technical problem was deficiencies in the welds connecting the steam generators to the reactor primary circuit. These were identified during construction and required years of investigation and repair before the nuclear safety regulator would permit the reactor to proceed to commissioning. Similar patterns applied to other systems across the project.

France has since ordered six EPR2 reactors, a next-generation standardised design, with a further eight under consideration. EDF has said lessons from Flamanville 3 could reduce costs on subsequent units by roughly 30%. The Cour des Comptes report that produced the €23.7 billion figure also warned this reduction is far from guaranteed, and recommended delaying the final investment decision on the EPR2 programme until financing is secured and design studies are further advanced.

The pattern across all five

Five projects, five different immediate causes of failure. BER collapsed because a public body without the right capability was given a project beyond its competence. Stuttgart 21 is being overtaken by time, regulation and inflation across a three-decade timeline. Olkiluoto and Flamanville ran into the consequences of allowing nuclear construction capability to lapse for a generation. Rail Baltica is carrying the coordination costs of executing a single project across three sovereign states with separate funding, planning and procurement systems.

The shared feature is not incompetence, though that was present in some cases. Every project on this list was approved on a cost estimate made before the project was defined in enough detail to produce an accurate figure. Once political approval was in place and construction had begun, the actual costs became visible. By then the sunk costs had accumulated and the political commitment existed. The projects continued.

Bent Flyvbjerg, whose research covers thousands of megaprojects across decades and geographies, has documented this dynamic consistently. The approval stage creates incentives to present numbers that will win the vote. The planning fallacy is not random error in the estimates. It is structural to how large projects get approved.
