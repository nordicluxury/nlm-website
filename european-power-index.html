From the report
Executive Summary

Europe is routinely described as a declining power, fragmented into twenty-seven competing national interests, dependent on the United States for security and on Asia for growth. That description is accurate in the language of geopolitics and false in the language of infrastructure.

One Dutch company is the only source on Earth of the machines that print advanced semiconductors. One Danish company and one privately held Swiss-Italian carrier move a combined third of the world's shipping containers. Two European drug makers supply most of the world's insulin.

None of this shows up on a market capitalisation table, because irreplaceability and size are different things.

Conclusion

Beneath Europe's political fragmentation sits a set of companies that the rest of the world, including the United States and China, cannot presently route around. No country can build an advanced chip without ASML and, one level further down, without Zeiss.

This is not a story about European strength in the way the term is normally used. It is a story about leverage that has gone largely unweaponised, held by companies rather than governments, and rarely coordinated with the political institutions nominally responsible for European strategic autonomy.

The gap this index reveals is not between Europe's power and its weakness. It is between the power Europe already has, sitting inside fifty boardrooms, and the political attention that power has so far received.

Methodology

Not market cap.
Irreplaceability.

Each company was assessed against four criteria designed to identify structural power — the kind that does not show up in a Fortune Global 500 or market capitalisation ranking but determines what happens to the world when something goes wrong.

  • Irreplaceability
    How quickly, and by whom, could the company's function be replaced if it vanished overnight?
  • Systemic dependency
    How deeply does the company sit inside infrastructure that others cannot easily exit — supply chains, energy grids, payment systems, defence networks?
  • Control of essentials
    Does the company govern access to food, energy, money, medicine, defence or communications at a scale that gives it leverage over governments or populations?
  • Bad actor power
    A deliberately uncomfortable criterion: what damage could the company realistically cause if it chose to act against the interests of those that depend on it? No company is accused of such intent. The criterion exists because the scale of potential harm is often the clearest evidence of how essential normal operation actually is.
